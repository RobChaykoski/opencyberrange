# Cyber Ethics, Privacy, and Digital Citizenship

This extra practice activity is a **decision activity**. It does not use RangeBox
commands, a terminal, or a network target. You will read short school scenarios,
identify authorization and privacy boundaries, and practise a calm
stop-and-report process.

## Before You Begin

### Where to run this activity

| Place | What it is | Used in this activity? |
|---|---|---|
| **Student device** | The laptop, desktop, or tablet in front of you. | Yes, for reading the page and writing notes. |
| **Web browser** | The app that shows this page. | Yes. |
| **Notes file** | Your own notes, outside RangeBox. | **Yes. This is where your decision records go.** |
| **RangeBox terminal** | The text window inside RangeBox. | **No. This activity uses no commands.** |
| **Assigned target** | A practice server named in a lab. | **None. This activity has no network target.** |
| **Public internet** | Real websites and services. | **No. Do not investigate real people or sites.** |
| **Classmates' accounts or devices** | Other people's information and equipment. | **No. Do not inspect, screenshot, or investigate them.** |

This activity has **no commands**, so there are no replaceable values or command
options to identify. If your teacher adds a lab to this discussion, use only the
target shown in that lab.

!!! warning "Use fictional examples"
    Do not collect, screenshot, or investigate real classmates' accounts,
    messages, photos, or personal information for this activity. Use the
    fictional scenarios on this page. If a real situation comes to mind, do not
    investigate it. Tell a trusted adult.

### What you need

- [ ] Your notes file.
- [ ] A pen or keyboard for writing short answers.
- [ ] About **20–30 minutes**.
- [ ] A teacher or trusted adult to talk to if a scenario raises a real concern.

### What you will be able to do

By the end of this activity, you will be able to:

- explain authorization, consent, privacy, personal information, credentials,
  evidence, responsible disclosure, and digital citizenship;
- tell the difference between "I can" and "I may";
- identify privacy boundaries in shared accounts, screenshots, messages, and
  accidental exposure;
- use a stop-and-report process;
- describe a concern without copying or exposing sensitive data;
- tell a mistake from intentional harm and choose a calm response.

## Words to Know

Read this table before you start.

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Authorization** | Permission to do a specific action on a specific system, from the person responsible. It is not the same as being able to do it. |
| **Consent** | A clear agreement from a person for their information or actions. Consent can be withdrawn. |
| **Privacy** | A person's control over who can see and use their information. |
| **Personal information** | Data that can identify a person, alone or combined, such as a name, photo, email address, phone number, location, or account handle. |
| **Credential** | A secret that proves identity and allows access, such as a password, PIN, token, or key. |
| **Evidence** | Information that shows what happened, such as a note or screenshot. Collect the minimum needed and share it only with a trusted adult through an approved channel. |
| **Responsible disclosure** | Reporting a problem privately to the right person so it can be fixed, without exploiting it or sharing it widely. |
| **Digital citizenship** | Using technology safely, respectfully, legally, and with care for other people. |
| **Redact** | Hide or remove sensitive details from a description or screenshot. |
| **Security incident** | An event that may harm systems or people, such as exposed data or unauthorized access. |
| **Trusted adult** | A teacher, counselor, principal, guardian, or other adult whose job is to help. |

## Authorization Is Permission, Not Ability

Being able to do something does not mean you are allowed to do it.

| You can… | But you may not… |
|---|---|
| Reach a practice target. | Use it outside the exercise that assigned it. |
| See that a class account is signed in. | Read the previous student's messages. |
| Open a file in a shared folder. | Copy or share it without permission. |
| Find a page on a school website. | Test it, scan it, or try to break it. |
| Take a screenshot. | Share someone else's private information. |

In a lab, authorization has three parts:

1. **The right target.** Use only the target shown in your exercise. Another
   student's lab, the school network, and the public internet are not authorized.
2. **The right action.** Use only the action the activity asks for. A
   connectivity check is not permission to scan, log in, or exploit.
3. **The right time.** Authorization applies to this activity. It does not carry
   over to another lab or another day.

A target being reachable is **not** authorization. A page being open is **not**
consent. A shared folder being visible is **not** permission to copy.

## Privacy Is About People

Privacy is not only about files. It is about people's control over their own
information.

| Situation | Privacy question | Safe habit |
|---|---|---|
| A shared classroom laptop is still signed in to another student's account. | Whose messages and files are these? | Do not read or copy them. Tell the teacher, who can secure the device. |
| A group chat contains a screenshot of a classmate's private message. | Did the classmate consent to sharing it? | Do not forward it. Tell a trusted adult. |
| A message contains a classmate's password. | Is this a credential that could let someone else in? | Do not repeat it. Tell the classmate and a trusted adult. |
| A student accidentally uploads a file with personal information to a shared folder. | Who can see it now? | Do not download or copy it. Tell the teacher. |
| A classmate asks for your password to "fix" your account. | Is this a safe request? | Do not share it. Use the official reset process. |

**Personal information** can be a single item, such as a photo, or a combination
of small items that together identify a person. Do not assume a detail is harmless
because it looks ordinary.

**Consent** is a clear yes. It can be withdrawn. A class account is not consent to
read someone else's messages, and a shared folder is not consent to copy
someone's work.

## Stop, Think, Report

Use this process when something feels wrong, private, or outside the activity.

### 1. Stop

Do not click, download, copy, forward, or investigate further. Do not try to
"prove" the problem by collecting more data.

### 2. Protect

Leave the device as it is if you are not sure. Do not delete files, messages, or
screenshots unless a trusted adult tells you to. Do not post about it in a group
chat.

### 3. Tell

Tell a trusted adult. In a lab, tell your teacher. For a school account or device,
tell your teacher or school IT staff. If the adult you would normally tell is
involved, tell another trusted adult.

### 4. Describe

Explain what happened without exposing passwords, private messages, or personal
information. Use roles instead of names when you can.

### 5. Follow up

Ask what to do next. Do not investigate alone. If you already shared something,
say so early so the adult can help.

## Who to Tell

| Situation | Who to tell |
|---|---|
| A lab target, command, or access problem | Your teacher. |
| A school account, device, or website problem | Your teacher or school IT staff. |
| A privacy problem involving another student | A teacher, counselor, or principal. |
| A problem involving a teacher or staff member | Another trusted adult, such as a counselor, principal, or guardian. |
| Immediate danger | A staff member, guardian, or emergency service. Do not handle it alone. |

If you are not sure who to tell, tell your teacher. If you are not sure whether
something is serious, ask. Asking is part of the work.

## What Not to Copy or Share

| Do not copy or share | Why |
|---|---|
| Passwords, PINs, tokens, or keys | A credential can let someone else into an account. |
| Names, photos, addresses, phone numbers, emails, or locations | These are personal information and can identify or locate someone. |
| Private messages, photos, or screenshots of other people | The person did not necessarily consent to sharing them. |
| More evidence than an adult needs | Collecting extra data increases the privacy risk. |
| Suspicious links or files | Forwarding them can spread the problem. |
| Another student's lab details or answers | That can break the exercise for them and may be outside your authorization. |

If an adult needs evidence, share only the smallest useful item through the
approved channel. **Redact** names, account details, and other sensitive details
when a redacted copy is enough.

## How to Describe an Issue Without Exposing Data

Use this template in your notes or when you speak to a trusted adult.

```text
What happened:
When it happened:
Where it happened (platform, room, or device role):
Who is affected (roles only, no private details):
What I saw (no passwords, private messages, or personal data):
What I did:
What I need:
```

**Example: shared laptop**

> On Tuesday during third period, a shared classroom laptop was signed in to a
> class account that was not mine. I saw a message list, but I did not open or
> copy anything. I locked the screen and told the teacher. I need help signing
> out safely.

**Example: possible website exposure**

> I noticed a school page that seems to list files that should not be public. I
> stopped, did not open the files, and did not share the link. I can show the
> teacher the page on my device.

These examples describe the issue without repeating a password, a private
message, or a person's name.

## Mistakes and Intentional Harm Are Not the Same

| | Mistake | Intentional harm |
|---|---|---|
| **What it looks like** | An accidental upload, a wrong link, a screen left open, a file shared with the wrong group. | Choosing to read, copy, share, or damage something that belongs to someone else, or ignoring a clear stop. |
| **What to do first** | Stop, tell a trusted adult, fix what can be fixed, and learn from it. | Stop, report it, and preserve evidence for the trusted adult. Do not confront the person alone. |
| **How to respond to the person** | Do not shame them. Help them correct the mistake. | Follow the school's safety process. Do not share the details widely. |
| **What it means** | A mistake is not the same as a plan to harm. | Intent and pattern matter. A repeated choice is different from an accident. |

A person who reports their own mistake is doing the responsible thing. Do not
hide a mistake to avoid embarrassment. Hiding it usually makes the problem
larger. Your teacher will explain what happens next; the goal is safety and
learning, not fear.

## Scenario Practice

For each scenario, write a **Decision Record** in your notes. Use only the
fictional details here. Do not investigate a real person.

| Scenario | What is the authorization or privacy issue? | What do you do first? | Who do you tell? | What do you not copy or share? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. A shared classroom laptop is signed in to the previous student's account. | | | | |
| 2. A group chat contains a screenshot of a classmate's private message. | | | | |
| 3. A message contains a classmate's password. | | | | |
| 4. A student accidentally uploads a file with personal information to a shared folder. | | | | |
| 5. You notice a school website page that may expose files it should not. | | | | |
| 6. A friend asks for your password to "fix" your account. | | | | |

### Strong Answer Guidance

A strong answer does not need to be long. It should include the safe first action
and the reporting step.

1. **Shared laptop:** do not read or copy the messages. Leave the device as it
   is and tell the teacher, who can secure it. Do not sign in as the other
   student.
2. **Private message screenshot:** do not forward it. Do not add comments in the
   group chat. Tell a trusted adult. If an adult needs evidence, share it only
   through the approved channel.
3. **Password in a message:** do not repeat the password. Do not screenshot it
   for the group. Tell the classmate to change it through the official process,
   and tell a trusted adult if it is a school account.
4. **Accidental upload:** do not download or copy the file. Tell the teacher so
   access can be removed. Do not shame the student; this is a mistake.
5. **Possible website exposure:** stop. Do not open the files, test the page, or
   share the link. Tell the teacher or school IT staff. Describe what you saw
   without collecting data.
6. **Password request:** do not share the password. Use the official reset or
   support process. Tell a trusted adult if the person keeps pressuring you.

## If You Are Unsure

Use these rules when a situation is not clear:

- If you are unsure whether you are allowed, **stop and ask before acting**.
- If you are unsure whether something is private, **treat it as private**.
- If you are unsure who to tell, **tell your teacher**. If the teacher is
  involved, tell another trusted adult.
- If you are unsure whether a detail is personal information, **leave it out** of
  your description.
- If you are unsure whether a file or link is safe, **do not open or forward
  it**.
- If someone is in immediate danger, tell a staff member, guardian, or emergency
  service. Do not handle it alone.

Uncertainty is not a failure. It is a signal to stop and ask.

## If Something Already Happened

If you clicked, opened, copied, or shared something before you realised it was
wrong:

1. **Stop.** Do not share it again or forward it to more people.
2. **Do not panic.** A calm report helps more than hiding the mistake.
3. **Do not delete evidence** unless a trusted adult tells you to. Keep it
   private and share only what is needed.
4. **Tell a trusted adult** what happened, including what you already did.
5. **If a credential was exposed,** change it through the official process and
   tell your teacher or school IT staff. Do not reuse the same password.
6. **If personal information was posted,** ask the teacher or IT staff for help
   removing it. Do not contact the person directly if that feels unsafe.
7. **Do not investigate the person** or try to fix the whole situation alone.

A mistake reported early is easier to fix than a mistake hidden for a long time.

## Key Takeaways

- Authorization is permission for a specific action on a specific system. Being
  able to do something is not permission to do it.
- Consent is a clear yes, and it can be withdrawn. A shared account or folder is
  not consent to read or copy.
- Privacy is about people's control over their information.
- Protect credentials and personal information. Do not copy or share more than a
  trusted adult needs.
- Stop, protect, tell, describe, and follow up. Do not investigate alone.
- Describe a concern without exposing passwords, private messages, or personal
  data.
- A mistake is different from intentional harm. Both need a calm response, but
  intentional harm needs the school's safety process.
- Responsible disclosure means reporting privately so a problem can be fixed,
  not exploiting it or sharing it widely.
- Digital citizenship means using technology safely, respectfully, legally, and
  with care for others.
